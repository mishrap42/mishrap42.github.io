Working Papers

The Global Allocative Efficiency of Deforestation

Job Market Paper

[Draft]

Deforestation releases carbon and yields agricultural profit; both vary sharply across space. Is deforestation occurring on the right land? Using global plot-level data and a general equilibrium trade model, I characterize the optimal geography of deforestation. At a $190 social cost of carbon, the Pigouvian policy avoids 56.2% of emissions since 2000 at an average cost of $1.30 per ton annually. A quarter of these gains require no food loss, coming from reallocating deforestation to lower-carbon forest. Without transfers, free-riding prevents optimal policy because tropical producers bear concentrated costs. Bilateral contracts deliver participation, but multilateral contracts can still unravel.

An Evaluation of Protected Area Policies in the European Union

with Tristan Grupp, Mathias Reynaert, and Arthur van Benthem

Review of Economic Studies, 2026 (advance access)

[Published Version] [PDF]

Media: VoxEU, Weekendavisen, Le Monde, Kleinman Center

The European Union (EU) designates 26% of its landmass as protected areas, limiting economic development for biodiversity. We use the staggered introduction of protected areas between 1985 and 2019 to study the selection of protected land and the causal effect of protection on vegetation cover and nightlights. We find no meaningful impacts on either outcome across four decades, countries, protection cohorts, or land characteristics. These null effects are consistent with the political economy of EU land protection: weak incentives to internalize biodiversity gains, green-glow motives, and area-based targets shape local siting and stringency choices. In practice, strict protection is applied where development pressure is low—so that protection has little bite—while in high-pressure regions, protection is typically weak, imposing only limited constraints on economic activity.

Trade-Offs and Synergies for Agriculture and Environmental Outcomes in the Tropics

with Jennifer Alix-Garcia, Juliano Assunção, Teevrat Garg, and Fanny Moffette

Review of Environmental Economics and Policy, 20(2), 2026, 149–173

[Published Version] [PDF]

Global population is currently 8 billion, projected to increase by 34 percent by 2050 and to plateau at 10.9 billion by 2100 (UN 2022). Feeding a growing population presents numerous challenges, including the possible need to expand land under agriculture at the expense of tropical forest and to increase the intensity of agricultural inputs such as water and fertilizer. Will feeding the world leave it with less forest, contaminated air and water, and decreased biodiversity? Are there pathways to food security that also conserve or improve environmental quality? This article compiles recent evidence on the relationship between agriculture and the environment, focusing on the tropics, where potential trade-offs between food production and environmental quality are particularly acute because much larger shares of the population depend upon agriculture for work and subsistence. Although land use is our primary metric of environmental impact, we also touch on associated ecosystem services such as soil and air quality.
